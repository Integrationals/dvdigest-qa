Part A: the business

A1. Insurance in five terms

Each term is defined before it is used again. The insurance lexicon holds them all, with more detail.

  • Carrier: an insurance company, also called the insurer. It issues the insurance contract, charges the premium for it (the price of the insurance, paid to keep the policy in force) [1], and handles and pays claims [1][2].
  • Policy: the insurance contract, which sets out when and how the carrier will compensate the person it covers [1].
  • Client: the person who buys a policy, called the insured or policyholder in insurance [1]. (The lexicon separates the insured, the owner and the beneficiary; this course says client until it needs those distinctions.)
  • Agent: a person licensed to solicit applications for, or negotiate, insurance policies [3]. To sell a carrier’s policies, an agent needs that carrier’s official authorization, called an appointment [4].
  • Insurance agency: a business that represents carriers and sells their insurance products [5]. The policies it sells remain the carrier’s contracts: an agent acts on the carrier’s behalf, and some agents, when a carrier authorizes it, may even issue or countersign its policies [6].

A2. The company: Digest Insurance Distributors

The course follows one fictional company, Digest Insurance Distributors, called the distributor from here on. It is modeled on a real insurance distributor in the United States, simplified to the parts the course uses.

The distributor is not a carrier: it does not write policies. It works with many carriers and sells their policies through a large network of agents and insurance agencies, and it handles contracting and back-office work for them. It earns a share of the commission on each policy sold, the commission being what the carrier pays for a sale: for life insurance, a percentage of the premium, largest in the policy’s first year [6]; for Medicare plans, a fixed amount per enrollment that Medicare caps every year [7]. A3. Why a distributor exists

Picture the industry without one. Every carrier would have to recruit, contract, license-check, train and pay thousands of independent agents one by one, each selling a modest number of policies. Every agent would have to contract separately with each carrier whose policies they want to sell, and would rarely sell enough of any one carrier’s policies to earn its better commission levels. In practice, carriers contracting with independent agents one by one is unusual [1].

A distributor sits in the middle. It holds contracts with many carriers and brings agents in under those contracts; it does not employ the agents and does not write policies [2]. The industry calls such a firm a marketing organization, under several names that mean much the same thing; this course says distributor.

For The distributor provides
Carriers One relationship that brings a whole network of agents, already contracted and trained; for Medicare plans, federal rules require that agents be trained and tested every year on Medicare rules and on the plans they sell [5]
Agents and agencies Many carriers’ policies through one relationship; better commission levels, because carriers pay by volume and the distributor’s combined volume earns rates no single agent reaches; marketing, sales leads, technology and back-office help

It earns an override: a share of each sale’s commission, paid by the carrier to the distributor on top of the agent’s commission. In one common arrangement the carrier splits the payment about 7 to 3 or 8 to 2, the agent keeping the larger share [3].

So the chain runs carrier, distributor, agency, agent, client, and each step in it exists because it is cheaper than the alternative (Figure A3-1).

Figure A3-1. The chain, and where the money goes. The policy is sold along the chain (solid arrows); the money takes a different route (dashed, marked $): the client pays the premium to the carrier, and the carrier pays the agent’s commission and the distributor’s override. This is the simplest common arrangement; depending on the contracts, commissions are sometimes paid through the distributor or the agency instead.

The same pattern in data. Without a middle, every carrier deals with every agency: N carriers times M agencies relationships, each with its own terms. With the distributor, each connects once: N plus M (Figure A3-2). Three carriers and three agencies show the shape: 9 contracts against 6. The gap grows with the network: 50 carriers and 2,000 agencies would need 100,000 direct contracts, against 2,050 through the middle. Data has the same problem and the same answer: a data integration layer that every source is mapped into once, and every report reads from once. This is a known pattern in data integration, the Canonical Data Model, from Hohpe and Woolf’s Enterprise Integration Patterns: a common model independent of any one application, so that adding a participant needs only one new translation, into the common model [4]. Their arithmetic counts translations among peers, every application talking to every other: six applications need 30 translations without the common model and 12 with it [4]. The distributor applies the same idea between two sides, carriers and agencies, and shows it is a business pattern as much as a data one. It is why, later in the course, we apply the Canonical Data Model to the vault: the vault keeps each agency’s records faithfully apart, and one common model in the middle integrates them, as the distributor does for the business.

A4. How it grows, and why that is a data problem

The distributor grows in two ways. Most of its agents are independent: they sell under contract with the distributor and remain their own businesses, and their sales reach the distributor as policies placed through it, recorded in its own systems as they happen. It also buys businesses outright, insurance agencies and smaller distributors, and that is what creates the data problem: each acquired business arrives with its own client records, kept in its own system, with its own way of numbering clients and years of history behind them. One agency numbers its clients A-1001, A-1002 and onward; another uses entirely different numbers; a person who was a client of two agencies appears in both, under two unrelated numbers.

The company needs every acquired agency’s records in one place, with nothing lost and every record traceable to where it came from. That is the job of the data vault this course builds: a way of storing data that keeps every source’s records, unchanged, with when and where each arrived. Lesson 1 explains what it does and what it does not do. Data Vault has two layers: the raw vault, which keeps the data sources send, and the business vault, which applies business rules on top of it [1][2]. Until the course introduces the business vault, the vault means the raw vault.

The first acquired agency in the course is Sunbelt Senior Insurance. Its nightly client list is the first data the vault receives.

A policy can name several people in different roles: the owner, who holds the contract; the insured, whose life or health it covers; and the payor, who pays its premium.

  1. How many distinct people are our clients, across every agency we have acquired?
  2. Which clients came to us through more than one agency?
  3. Which policies does each client hold, with which carriers, and in which roles: owner, insured or payor?
  4. How much business does each carrier write through us?
  5. Which agent wrote each policy, and where do our sources disagree about it?
  6. Which independent agents sell through us without an agency?
  7. Where did this client’s record come from, and when did we first know it?
  8. What did we know about this policy on a given date, and what has changed since?

  10. When we acquire the next agency, how much of its book already overlaps ours?

They split in two. Questions about where a record came from, when it arrived and what changed (7, 8 and 9), and about who holds which policy with which agent (3 and 5), are what the vault keeps by design, from the first lessons on. Questions that need one person recognized across different numbers (1, 2 and 10) cannot be answered by the vault alone: they need the data integration layer of section A3, built later in the course. Questions 4 and 6 need a measure or a rule the sources do not carry yet. Each lesson moves at least one of the ten forward.

A6. What the course builds

Before the lessons take it apart piece by piece, here is the whole of what the course builds, in two pictures: the source data as it arrives, and the vault built from it. Both are entity relationship diagrams (ERDs). In an ERD each table is a box listing its columns, and each relationship is a line between two boxes. The marks at a line’s ends say how many rows on one side can meet one row on the other: a bar means exactly one, a circle means none at all is allowed, and the three-pronged foot, called a crow’s foot, means many. A line is dashed when nothing declares the relationship and it holds only where the values happen to match.

Part B: how this course works

B1. Four terms from software teams

The course is built the way a software team builds things, so four of its terms come from that world. Each is used only after this point.

  • Repository: the place that holds all of a project’s files and stores each file’s revision history [1]. The course’s vault is built from one repository.
  • Commit: one recorded change to one or more files, with its author and a short message describing the change [1].
  • Branch: a parallel version of a repository, so work in progress stays apart until it is merged [1]. Each branch drifts from the other as commits land on it; a merge joins one branch’s commits into another. Finished work merges into a branch named qa, where it is tested, and is then promoted to the branch named main, which production builds from.
  • GitHub: the website that hosts the course’s repository [1].

B3. How a part runs

  1. The teacher builds the part’s piece of the vault in the course’s repository, under the same delivery process a professional team follows: documents first, tests before code, then the build.

Each lesson is read one at a time; the learner’s questions while reading become edits, applied together at the lesson’s end, before the next lesson is written.

2. What the vault does

Three terms describe what happened, each defined here before it is used again.

  • Source: the system a row comes from. Here there is one source, Sunbelt Senior’s system, which the vault identifies by a short permanent code, SSI.
  • History: the record of when each thing arrived. In this part of the course, history means the moment a client number was first seen; later parts add how a client’s details change over time.
  • Lineage: the record of where each thing came from. Every client number the vault keeps carries the code of its source.

So the instance shows the vault’s whole job, stated generally: the vault keeps every client number each source sends, with its history and its lineage, and it never changes or removes what it has kept [5]. A-1007 and A1007 are both kept, exactly as written, each with the time it arrived and the source that sent it.

3. What the vault does not do

The vault does not decide that A-1007 and A1007 belong to the same person. It cannot: the two numbers are written differently, and nothing in the source data says they refer to one client. A person looking at the rows sees the same name and suspects a typo; the vault sees two client numbers.

The same holds more widely. The vault does not combine Sunbelt Senior’s clients with another agency’s clients, and it does not correct values it believes are wrong. All three are decisions about what the data means, and the people who wrote the Data Vault method place every such decision after the vault, not inside it [6].

This is the question the course keeps returning to. It is tempting to think of the vault as the place where data from many sources comes together. What it actually does is keep each source’s data apart, faithfully and with its history and lineage. Deciding that two kept records describe one client, and combining them, is separate work that comes after.

4. A worked example

2. Four terms

  • Business key: the identifier a source’s business uses for a thing, the way its people use it. Here it is the client number: the vault’s whole job, in lesson 1’s words, is to keep every client number each source sends. The toolkit that builds this course’s vault takes business keys as the input of everything that follows [3].
  • Staging: the vault’s first step, one per source table, which reads the landed rows and prepares them for the vault without changing what they mean [4]. Here it is the view stg_sunbelt_client, over SUNBELT.CLIENT. It holds 45 rows: the 43 rows Sunbelt Senior sent, and two rows the vault adds itself, which lesson 5 explains [2].
  • Hard rule: a change to how a value is stored that leaves its meaning alone: casting text to a timestamp, trimming spaces, turning an empty value into no value. The raw vault applies hard rules and nothing else; changes of meaning, called soft rules, belong to the business vault after it [5]. Lesson 2’s load timestamp, converted from text to a timestamp, was a hard rule.
  • Hash key: a code of fixed length computed from a business key, which the vault then uses to identify that key [3]. Here it is 32 characters computed from the client number by a standard algorithm, MD5 [6].
Check yourself

Turning an empty client number into no value: hard rule or soft rule, and why?

Show the answerHide the answer

A hard rule: it changes how the value is stored, not what it means. Soft rules change meaning and belong to the business vault.

3. How the hash key is made, and what it ignores

The toolkit trims the spaces around the client number, writes it in capitals, puts it between quotation marks and computes its MD5 [6][7]. For A-1001, Rosa’s number, that is the MD5 of “A-1001”, which is 2eec4d22ff4ec7df061cc883ac8d1b04; Rosa’s row in the vault carries exactly that key [8]. Any reader can repeat the calculation.

Because the number is written in capitals before it is hashed, a-1004 and A-1004 give one key; because spaces around it are trimmed, A-1005 with a space and A-1005 without give one key. Both are hard rules: letter case and surrounding spaces do not change which client a number names, and the toolkit is set to treat business keys that way [6]. The hash key does not remove the hyphen, though. A1007 and A-1007 give two keys, as lesson 1 showed, because deciding that they name one client is a decision about meaning, and that is not the raw vault’s to make.

The empty values needed a rule of their own. Without it, an empty client number would be hashed like any other value and become a key that looks real but names no client. When the course first built staging without the rule, a test showed exactly that: the empty value hashed to a key beginning 9d4568c0 [9]. With the rule, staging turns an empty or space-only client number into no value first, and every row with no client number receives the same reserved key, 32 zeros. What the vault does with that key is lesson 5’s subject.

Check yourself

Show the answerHide the answer

The hash key ignores letter case and the spaces around a number, and nothing else. A missing hyphen is a different spelling; deciding that A1007 is A-1007 is a decision about meaning, which the raw vault does not make.

Criterion 4 needs a reference: the teacher’s list of findings a strong reviewer should catch, called the answer key. The teacher writes it before the review and posts a fingerprint of its exact text (a SHA-256 hash) on the course’s master issue. At grading the key is opened and fingerprinted again. If even one character changed, the fingerprints would, with very high probability, differ [5]. So the teacher cannot add findings after seeing the review, and the learner knows the grading was fixed in advance.

Appendix 1: Questions for a Data Vault architecture conversation
Course DIGEST Data Vault Course, appendix 1

The course’s conclusions, gathered in one place: how to frame the course’s argument, the questions it leads to, the terms they use, and how far the course’s own vault has been built.

The framing, in two sentences

Data Vault keeps every source’s history faithfully, with full lineage; on its own it does not decide what a client, a policy or a status is across sources. This course finishes an approach John Giles began, the Universal Data Vault: hubs built from universal data model patterns (appendix 2), completed with the patterns Giles set aside (party, party role, party relationship and role within a transaction), as Silverston’s universal data model patterns define them. It is the Canonical Data Model applied inside the vault: one common model for what things are, which every source is translated into once.

Why “canonical” matters: the Canonical Data Model is Hohpe and Woolf’s pattern from Enterprise Integration Patterns. With a common model, adding a participant needs only one new translation; by their count, six applications need 30 translations without one and 12 with it.

The ten questions

Ask them as an architect learning how a team handles each case, not as objections.

# Ask What it rests on
1 When a source sends sales with an ISBN, where does your team decide that a book is a concept the business tracks, and who owns that decision? The raw vault creates hubs for concepts no source sent; that is a business decision
2 When two sources identify the same thing with different keys, where in your architecture are they made one? A hub only matches identical key strings; it reuses sameness an issuer already created
3 Where does your team’s integrated view of a client live, and how many places define “client”? Integration deferred past the vault is not removed; done per mart it multiplies
4 How does your team integrate ten carriers’ policy statuses and product categories into one view? The business vault resolves identity at best; status and classification need typed rows
5 How does your team absorb a source whose entities change month to month, and how long does remapping take? The raw vault cannot load a table until its entities are decided
6 Where is source history kept before the vault, and how are full-snapshot sources reduced to versions? Toolkits and practitioners (Datavault Builder, Roelant Vos) place a persistent staging area beside the vault
7 If an auditor asks for a source table exactly as it arrived on a given day, how is it produced? Once staging is cleared, the source’s own table must be re-assembled from hubs, links and satellites
8 When a modeling decision in the raw vault turns out wrong, how is history repaired? Sources without change capture cannot be reloaded
9 How are hundreds of columns assigned to hubs, links and satellites, and what happens to a link when a source adds a code? A satellite has one parent; a new code means a new link
10 What part of your vault’s build is automated, and what part is still decided by people? Generators write the objects after people make the decisions
11 How does your team key a source row that arrives without its business key, and where does that row’s history live? A shared zero key collides in a satellite’s key; keeping the row in persistent staging and raising a finding keeps it without a false entity

The likely objection: performance

The answer: query speed is the marts’ job, in this design as in Data Vault. The data integration layer’s cost is the build, and the expensive joins are built in stages as materialized steps, incremental with change data capture, run in parallel.

Point Support
Consumers query marts, not the data integration layer Data Vault itself is described as a data integration layer, not a consumption layer; teams that query the vault directly find its joins unwieldy [1]
Generalized, pattern-based structures can be built at enterprise scale Giles built them for 83 merged organizations and about 8,000 source tables (appendix 2); his articles give no timings
Build cost is managed by staging A large join is materialized in parts (ten tables as two five-table steps, then combined), each build processes only changes, and independent steps run in parallel: practice from the author’s own work on IMDb data, to be shown with timings as the course’s builds use it

Prior art: the Universal Data Vault

John Giles published a way to combine Silverston’s universal data model patterns with Data Vault and named it the Universal Data Vault [1]. It puts the patterns into the vault itself: generalized hubs such as Asset, Activity and Agreement hold every subtype, each subtype a row in a hand-made type table, with the type and the business code together making a key unique. He set party and party role aside as a possibly contentious topic, adding that the UDV structures could accommodate the alternative modeling choices [2].

This course builds on that design rather than departing from it. Its hubs are universal too, and its contribution is the part Giles left aside: party, party role, party relationship and role within a transaction, taken from Silverston’s universal data model patterns. Its first example is one hub for people, keyed by issuer and identifier, with a person-policy link that carries the role.

Appendix 2: Study of John Giles’s Universal Data Vault
Course DIGEST Data Vault Course, appendix 2
Sources John Giles, “Universal Data Vault: Case Study in Combining ‘Universal’ Data Model Patterns with Data Vault Architecture”, TDAN, Part 1 (https://tdan.com/?p=19072) and Part 2 (https://tdan.com/?p=19245), both read in full on 2026-10-24
Method Sections 1 to 6 report what Giles wrote, paraphrased. Section 7 is the course’s own reasoning, labeled as such. Section 8 lists what the articles do not settle.

1. Who he is and what he set out to do

Giles is an independent consultant in information architecture, working in IT since the late 1960s, a Fellow of the Australian Computer Society, and the author of The Nimble Elephant: Agile Delivery of Data Models Using a Pattern-based Approach (Part 1, author note). He asks whether combining universal data model patterns with Data Vault keeps the benefits of both or cancels them out (Part 1, opening).

2. The case that produced it

  • A government order merged 83 related Australian organizations into one on July 1, each with about 5 core systems of about 20 central entities: an estimated 8,000 core tables (Part 1).
  • He first built a pattern-based enterprise logical data model (ELDM), after a one-day patterns course for business and IT people; the business shaped and owned it, as specializations of generic patterns (Part 1).
  • The data warehouse had to consolidate the history of all 83 organizations, serve reporting and operational queries, and give one consistent view of a client who appeared in several of them (Part 1).
  • Earlier Kimball-style marts had cross-domain problems, and an Inmon-style warehouse was under way; he proposed Data Vault for auditability and adaptability, and a skeptical senior developer later found it lived up to its claims (Part 1).
  • His earlier experience: he built a telecommunications ELDM in two weeks by starting from one of Silverston’s industry patterns (Part 1).

3. The dilemma

  • Universal patterns are deliberately generalized, and Silverston and Agnew’s third volume notes that the generalized forms are what is usually implemented (Part 1).
  • Data Vault practitioners usually recommend hubs that avoid high generalization, at the level the business speaks (Part 1).
  • People speak at different levels: his wildfire example runs from “deployable resources” through “fire trucks” and “aircraft” to “water tankers”, “slip-ons”, “helicopters” and “fixed-wing planes” (Part 1).
  • With one agreed level, standard hubs work. In the merged organization there was no single level; each group wanted its own (Part 1).
  • Two mandates followed: the vault must accept back-loaded history from all 83 organizations without hundreds of hubs, and it must align with the one generalized ELDM shared by the service bus, master data, reference data and business rules work (Part 1).
  • He notes the standard alternative, a hub per level joined by same-as links recording inheritance, and says it should be considered, but that here it risked hundreds of hubs (Part 1).

4. The design

Construct What Giles specifies
Generalized hub One hub per pattern (his examples: Asset, Activity; also Account, Agreement, Event, Location, Product), holding every subtype; named from the ELDM’s chosen patterns (Part 2)
Hub type table A self-referencing table of subtypes and their parent and child hierarchy (Helicopter and Fixed-Wing Plane under Aircraft); configuration data written by the design team, not a Data Vault table, with no source or load date; a new subtype is a new row, not a new hub (Part 2)
Abstract or concrete flag Marks types used only to group others; optional (Part 2)
Hub uniqueness Type plus business code, because a generalized hub could hold an aircraft and a truck with the same registration number (Part 2)
Generalized link Links generalized the same way, each row pointing to its link type; his banking example: signatory, guarantor, witness and approver links between parties and agreements (Part 2)
Link type table Defines each allowed specialized link between generalized hubs; configuration, not a Data Vault table; new link types added as rows (Part 2)
Roles on self-referencing links Where it is unclear which side contains the other (truck and crew, office block and treatment plant), the link type table names each participant’s role (Part 2)
Satellites Regular Data Vault satellites, which can hang at any level of the hierarchy and on links (Part 2)
Rules Kept as metadata, such as expected cardinality in link types, used to report breaches of loaded data, never to exclude data from the load (Part 2)
Visualization Structure held as data is harder to see; he suggests generating XMI files from the type tables for UML tools (Part 2)

5. What he set aside and what he found hard

  • Party and party role: a possibly contentious topic he leaves aside, saying other modeling choices fit the UDV structures (Part 2).
  • Business keys: hard to find in regular Data Vault, harder for generalized hubs (Part 2).
  • Visibility: structure in type tables is less familiar to many modelers (Part 2).
  • A cautionary story via Graham Witt about bottom-up modeling, a school database that assumed parents share a student’s address, with serious consequences; he argues starting from generalized patterns would have exposed it (Part 2).

6. His results and guidance

  • He reports that over 90 percent of a colleague’s most complex sample data fitted the patterns, and that the first project’s success led a former client employee to ask him to repeat it elsewhere (Part 2).
  • His guidance: if the enterprise has one sweet spot on the generalization continuum, use standard Data Vault; if a few focal points, use hubs for each with same-as links; if it values a multi-level hierarchy, especially pattern-based, consider the UDV (Part 2).

7. The course’s reading (the course’s reasoning, not Giles’s)

  1. The course’s position is universal hubs inside the vault, as Giles’s are; it finishes his approach rather than offering an alternative. Provisional: this rests on the two articles; his later Data Vault book (section 9) includes a Party pattern, and until it is read the course does not claim he left party unfinished. Increment 2’s design shows it: one hub for people keyed by issuer and identifier (type-plus-code uniqueness, in Giles’s terms), the policy hub qualified by carrier, and the person-policy link carrying the role, close to his link types that name each participant’s role.
  2. What it adds: party, party role, party relationship and role within a transaction, from Silverston’s universal data model patterns, the area Giles set aside. By his own words the UDV structures can hold those choices, so adding them completes rather than corrects him.
  3. What he solves and the course keeps: specialization as rows, not new hubs, which partly answers the course’s contradictions 4 and 9.
  4. His hub type tables have no source or load date; the course’s source system seed makes the same move.
  5. Open: contradictions 1, 5 and 8 say entity decisions made at load are costly when wrong. Universal hubs reduce how often a new hub is needed, but still decide at load which pattern and type a row belongs to. How the course answers that is not settled.

10. First finding from The Elephant in the Fridge

In chapter 2, “Untangling ‘raw’ and ‘business’ Data Vault,” Giles separates two questions: whether a hub, link or satellite’s structure reflects the business view or a raw source view, and whether each instance, by its record source, came from a raw source feed or from a business rule. He suggests avoiding both phrases, and noting structure and sourcing instead. For integrating satellites, he describes one source-shaped satellite per feed plus a business-centric “conformed” satellite.
